VISTA: making an AI HR tool usable before it shipped to 1,000 people

A product team was about to roll a vibe-coded HR platform out to the whole company with no onboarding and no UX input. I turned a team that didn't want UX into one that commissioned three rounds of research, led the redesign of the core flows, and the redesigned tool shipped company-wide.

In short

  • VISTA is an internal HR platform: AI-assisted OKR drafting and alignment, plus an AI-upskilling module that scores employees and builds them a growth plan. It was heading for a no-onboarding launch to 1,000+ staff.
  • I was pulled in late and narrow, for a quick look at a beta another team had built. The manager didn't think UX was worth the time.
  • A live, screen-by-screen teardown plus a working redesign prototype changed that. The team went from declining UX to funding three rounds of usability research and taking the case to their own director.
  • We benchmarked against the industry-standard SUS average. The beta scored below it, which is exactly what proved the work was needed. Iteration lifted the core flow; the redesigned tool shipped company-wide.

Walkthrough

The Figma Make prototype of the redesigned flow: a status toggle, a clearer "Validate with AI" action, locked inputs during validation, and feedback grouped with the field it refers to. Shared straight with the dev team to show the changes, not describe them.

What I owned: the heuristic diagnosis, the redesign direction, the working prototype, and the usability research. I didn’t write or ship the production code. I owned the UX of the last mile.

The problem, and what was at risk

VISTA was going out to the entire company with no onboarding, by design. That single decision made intuitiveness the whole game: if the tool wasn’t self-explanatory, there was no safety net.

The beta wasn’t ready for that. Onboarding was effectively a giant user-guide document where everything lived, the kind no one reads. Progressive disclosure was barely present. And there were UX problems threaded through the core flows that, at the scale of a thousand-plus people, don’t stay small. An unintuitive internal tool shipped cold to everyone doesn’t just annoy people; it generates a wave of confusion and rework, and it quietly undermines the thing VISTA existed to do, get people comfortable using AI in their work.

The harder problem sat above all of that. The team owned the build and the timeline, and the manager didn’t want to spend days on UX. So before any design problem, there was a credibility problem: proving UX was worth the time to someone who’d already decided it wasn’t.

Constraints

No onboarding at launch, so the burden fell entirely on the interface. A ship date the business wouldn’t move. A capable dev team, but developers under deadline pressure, who reliably miss UX detail not because they can’t see it but because it isn’t their job to. And me: an outside advisory function on someone else’s build, with no mandate, walking into a room that hadn’t asked for me.

What I decided, and what I didn’t

I diagnosed live, in the room, rather than sending a report. The obvious move was a written heuristic audit. I chose to sit with the PM and dev team and walk their beta screen by screen, naming what broke and why, because sceptics need to see the problems, not read about them. That decision is what earned the first shift in the room.

Three of the fixes are worth naming, because they show the reasoning, not just the result:

  • Recognition over recall. Icons were carrying the wrong meaning, either their form implied one thing or their placement implied a function they didn’t perform. Users were being asked to remember what things did instead of recognising it. I rebuilt them around recognisable, correctly-placed affordances.
  • Consistency. The same UI element appeared in different places doing different things, which quietly poisons mental-model formation and invites error, users learn a pattern in one place and it betrays them in the next. I made element behaviour consistent so one learning transferred across the tool.
  • Grouping AI feedback to cut cognitive load. The AI’s feedback on each Key Result was dumped in a single block at the bottom of the OKR module, so users had to work out which comment belonged to which KR. I regrouped each piece of feedback with its Key Result, using proximity to do the work the user was being forced to do in their head.

I killed the manual rather than improving it. The beta’s onboarding model was one enormous guide document containing everything. The obvious fix was to make the guide better. I replaced it with progressive disclosure and patterns borrowed from tools people already used, because no one reads the manual, which makes the manual the wrong artefact to fix.

And I proved it with a working prototype, not a deck. I came back days later not with recommendations but with the full analysis and a live, clickable redesign built in Figma and Figma Make, showing the improved flows mapped to what users were actually trying to do, in OKR creation and in the AI training assistant. The recommendations deck was the safe option. The working artefact is what turned “we’ll consider it” into “help us test this properly.”

The part that mattered: turning a team that didn’t want UX

This is the real story, and it’s not about the pixels.

The manager didn’t want UX at all. A developer I’d worked with before flagged the beta, and I offered a no-strings look. The in-room teardown got the first lightbulbs, the audible “we missed that, we missed that.” But it was the follow-up days later, the full analysis plus the working prototype, that actually moved them. The developer told me directly that the insights were genuinely useful, and the PM who owned the project was impressed enough by the first round to ask my team back to run the usability testing.

Then the bit that makes this a lead story rather than a nice anecdote: they took it upstream themselves. The team used the value we’d shown to go to their own director and secure his buy-in to fund the research, their argument being the one I’d made to them, get it right the first time or pay for it in support load and rework later. I didn’t just win the room. The room went and won the next room for me. For a designer with no mandate walking into a project that didn’t want them, that second-order influence is the outcome I’d point to first.

How we worked: AI for speed, judgment stayed human

The efficiency came from AI; the rigour didn’t. That distinction is the whole point.

For the redesign, Figma Make let us generate revised pages fast enough that the dev team saw exactly what was meant with no interpretation rounds in between. Turnaround on a complex redesign dropped from roughly two or three weeks to a day or two. For the research, we used ChatGPT and Gemini to cluster the qualitative feedback, work through SUS, and draft the observations-insights-recommendations structure, taking raw data to a shareable report from around three weeks to about one.

What AI didn’t do was the judgment. On review it drifted, over-reading the data, pulling themes a shade too far, and I caught and corrected that on every pass. Being physically in the room moderating the sessions gave us context no model had access to. The honest version of the AI story isn’t “it made us faster.” It’s “it made us faster and I stayed the editor, because a theme the model over-reads is a recommendation you can’t defend.”

What happened

The redesigned tool shipped company-wide. The clearest proof it mattered isn’t a single number, it’s the behaviour: a team that had declined UX ended up funding three rounds of research and shipping the redesign to over a thousand people.

On the measured side, I’ll be precise, because the numbers are honest and the honesty is the point. We benchmarked with SUS, where the industry average sits at 68 (the 50th percentile across hundreds of studies, not a pass mark, and not a percentage). The beta came in below that average, and that below-benchmark score was the diagnostic win: it’s the evidence that made the case for UX to a team that hadn’t wanted to spend the time. On the OKR creation flow, iteration across two rounds moved SUS from 63 to 65.8, closing on the average. The AI upskilling module, tested once, scored 58.1, which flagged clearly where the next round of iteration needed to go.

That next round didn’t happen. The ship date won, and the upskilling module went out without the iteration cycle the OKR flow got. Which is the honest limitation of this project, and it leads straight into what I’d change.

What I’d do differently

Measurement has to ship with the product. I’d have liked one more validation round before rollout to confirm the direction on the upskilling module, or failing that, a round after launch to find where it needed work. Neither was possible against the business timeline. The lesson I took, and it’s one I’ve now seen twice, is to bake the measurement into the plan early and agree it as a condition of the work, so the schedule can’t quietly eat the part that tells you whether the design actually landed.
