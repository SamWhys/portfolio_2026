02 Case study

An AR sales tool that put the product in the room, not on a truck

Sales teams were shipping large-format display units around the world to win early-stage conversations. I designed an internal AR tool that put the product in the client's space instead, scoped to an MVP that survived regional politics, validated with the sales reps who'd use it, and shipped as a shared platform for global sales teams.

In short

  • Demonstrating large-format displays meant shipping physical units: expensive, slow, and useless for showing scale and fit in the client's own space.
  • I designed an internal mobile AR tool that let reps place products into real environments mid-conversation, and generate a custom sales sheet or book a demo from the same flow.
  • Limited budget and competing regional demands forced a real scoping decision: an MVP around the highest-value demo flows, with everything else phased, not launch-blocking.
  • Tested with internal sales users before development: both test missions completed by 4 of 5 and 5 of 5 testers, and 5 of 8 survey respondents said they'd use it regularly.

Discover The demo had a physics problem

Early-stage sales conversations for large-format displays had a physics problem. Shipping a demo unit to a prospect was expensive, slow, and environmentally inefficient, and a static image can’t answer the question the client is actually asking: what does this look like at scale, on my wall, in my space? Every shipped pallet was cost and delay spent before anyone knew whether the conversation was serious, and every conversation without hardware leaned on imagination.

I started with the people who lived inside that problem: sales stakeholders across regions. Discovery discussions broke down how teams actually demonstrated large-format products, where the existing process failed, and what an AR tool would have to support in a real, live conversation. Three priorities came out of that work:

  • Demonstrate product scale and placement without shipping physical units.
  • Be simple enough to use during a live client conversation.
  • Prioritise the product categories and demo flows with the highest sales value first.

Discovery also surfaced what could kill the project, and that shaped what came next more than anything else.

Define Narrow on purpose

Three constraints framed the definition. Budget: resources covered the highest-value demo flows, not the catalogue. Geography: regional teams wanted different product lines first, and each had reasons. Scepticism: some teams wouldn’t commit resources before seeing proof the tool could hold up in a real sales conversation; the argument had to be won with an artefact, not a proposal.

So the problem statement got narrow on purpose: let a rep place the real product, at real scale, into the client’s actual room, mid-conversation, and let the same flow end in a custom sales sheet or a booked demo rather than a dead stop.

The defining decision was scope. The pressure was to launch with every region’s products represented; the decision was to phase: ship the flows and categories that made the strongest demos first, and treat additional lines as roadmap, not launch blockers. The phased roadmap was as much a stakeholder-management artefact as a planning one: it let me say yes to everyone about eventually while protecting what shipped first. That’s what let the project exist at all inside its budget.

Ideate Confidence, not novelty

With the scope set, the open question was interaction: what should AR feel like inside a sales conversation? I reviewed a range of mobile AR apps across product visualisation, object placement, onboarding, and spatial scanning, looking for the patterns that matter when the person holding the phone is mid-pitch. Five findings became the interaction rules:

  • Users need clear guidance before entering AR mode.
  • Placement has to feel immediate and forgiving.
  • Controls stay minimal during live demos.
  • Product information must be reachable without leaving the AR view.
  • The experience needed to support confidence, not novelty.

The last one is the thesis. In a live demo, novelty is a liability; the rejected alternative was the show-off version of AR, and rejecting it was the point: the tool’s job is to keep the rep’s conversation moving. These rules shaped the flow structure, the onboarding, and the AR interaction model.

Then I mapped the core demo flows: select a product category and SKU, enter AR, place the product in the client’s space, reach product details, compare or switch products mid-demo, and exit into a custom sales sheet or a booked demo.

Design The rules become the interface

The flows became wireframes, and the wireframes became an interactive prototype of the full loop, built to find out whether the flow held up for a rep in a live client conversation before further development effort was committed.

The first-run experience carries the “guidance before AR” rule: an eight-step walkthrough that runs from choosing a product through scanning the wall, placing the product, capturing a photo and generating a salesheet, with a skip control on every screen for a rep who has seen it before.

The working screens carry the rest of the rules: a product list with expandable cards under three category tabs, product detail one tap deep, and the two conversion exits the flow promises: a custom sales sheet generated and emailed from inside the tool (with a success state that says exactly where it went), and a book-a-demo form.

Design didn’t stop at screens. Components were documented with anatomy, states, and layout specs for the development handoff; the top-navigation component below is one of nine component specifications on that board.

The handoff was not a drop. Through implementation I stayed with the development team to keep the design intent intact while the build met its real constraints: how AR behaved on the device, how product assets were handled, and what held up on a phone. In practice that meant clarifying interaction states, reviewing builds, documenting edge cases, and simplifying flows where a constraint made the original version unreasonable. Regular check-ins with engineering and with regional stakeholders kept the thing pointed at the sales goals it started from.

Validate Tested before anyone built

The prototype went in front of internal sales users with structured missions before anyone wrote production code, because the cost of a wrong flow drops by an order of magnitude before development. Both missions completed (4 of 5 and 5 of 5 testers) and the misses were exactly the useful kind. What they located was hesitation at AR setup, and the places where navigation, labelling and the AR entry point had to be simplified before a rep could keep a conversation moving. Those simplifications landed before the build started.

The honest frame for these numbers: 5 testers and 8 survey respondents is a small internal sample, treated as directional input for iteration rather than proof. That is what the test was for; 5 of 8 respondents said they’d use the tool regularly, and the same survey said when: trade shows, new prospects, earlier in the sales process, each picked by three quarters of the respondents who got that far. Closing a deal came last, at half. Reps read this as an early-funnel tool, which is exactly what the MVP had been scoped to be. That’s adoption intent with a context attached, which is more useful to a roadmap than a bigger number without one.

The least flattering number is worth printing too. Testers also rated how intuitive each flow felt on a five-point scale: on the first mission one of the five put it at the very bottom, and on the second none fell below the middle. These were two missions inside one session rather than a before and an after, so the second is not evidence that the first was fixed. It is just what the room thought at the time.

Results & learnings What happened, and what held up

The tool shipped and became a shared platform for global sales teams, and the clearest adoption signal was the queue of regions asking to get more of their catalogue into it. Post-launch feedback ran the same direction the scoping fight had: more SKUs, more categories, more region-specific scenarios, including one business unit asking for a whole new product category in the tool’s taxonomy. The structure absorbed those requests without redesigning the core experience, which is what the phased roadmap was for.

On the numbers, the honest version: the usability results above are real, small-sample, and were used as iteration input rather than victory laps. And the business reported a $10M USD revenue increase in the first three months post-launch with the tool supporting upselling during demos. I treat that figure carefully and it does not appear in this study’s evidence ledger: I didn’t own the attribution model, so it’s a reported business outcome I contributed to, not a measurement I can defend line-by-line. The operational outcome I can stand fully behind: early-stage conversations stopped requiring a shipped pallet, along with the cost and the environmental load of moving one.

What this project taught me, grounded in what actually happened:

  • The roadmap was a design artefact. The phasing decision did more to ship this product than any screen: it converted regional pressure into sequence instead of scope.
  • Testing before building is the cheap kind of correct. Navigation, labelling, and AR entry were all fixed for the cost of a prototype round, not a development cycle.
  • Conversation-first held up. Post-launch requests asked for more catalogue inside the same experience, not changes to the core flow. The restraint in the AR interaction model was the right bet.
