03 Case study

UNIVERSE: a launch site and design system for an education metaverse

A brand-new education SaaS needed a public face before launch. I designed it end to end (flows, wireframes, and a full component system in four languages) and replaced a signup path that dumped buyers at a sign-in wall with a purchase journey that took a school from pricing page to running its first class.

Role
UX/UI Designer
Team
Me on UX/UI, with the product, brand, and development teams
Where & when
A display-hardware manufacturer, 2022
Scope
Public marketing site + design system for a SaaS launch, 4 languages

In short

  • UNIVERSE is an education-metaverse SaaS: 3D classrooms, spatial audio, teaching tools. The site had to explain a new product category and convert two audiences: try-for-free teachers and buy-now schools.
  • The original signup flow ended in the wrong places: pricing led to a sign-in wall, three different entry points converged inconsistently. I redesigned it as one continuous journey: account, verification, payment, student import, app download, first sign-in.
  • I built the design system alongside the pages (type scale, buttons, navigation, footers, CTA blocks, localisation into four languages) so a fast-moving launch surface stayed consistent.
  • Product decisions were still moving while the site was designed (trial length and pricing were both in flux), so the flows had to absorb change without redesign.

What I owned: the flows, the wireframes, the visual system, and the handoff. The product, its pricing, and its trial policy belonged to the product team.

Anonymised: the employer’s name is withheld; the product is public and keeps its name. Everything else is as it happened.

The problem, and what was at risk

UNIVERSE launched into a category most buyers had never purchased from: a metaverse platform dedicated to education. The site carried a double burden: explain an unfamiliar product credibly enough for schools to spend budget on it, and make trying it frictionless enough that individual teachers would self-serve. A confusing path on either side wasn’t a cosmetic problem; for a brand-new SaaS, the site effectively was the sales team.

Constraints

The product was pre-launch and still moving: trial length and pricing were both live questions while the site was being designed; the artefacts show a seven-day trial in one place and a ten-day trial being explored in another. The site also had to ship in four languages (English, Traditional Chinese, Spanish, German) and hold together visually while pages were added at launch speed.

What I decided, and what I didn’t

I rebuilt the buy flow as a journey, not a button. The original flow had the classic pre-launch shape: entry points bolted on one at a time; pricing led to a sign-in wall, “try for free” and “learn more” converged on the same account screen from different directions, and nothing past account creation was designed at all. The rejected option was to patch it, entry by entry. Instead I mapped the full journey a school actually takes (choose a package, create an account, verify email, pay, get the invoice, import students, download the app, sign in) and designed each step as part of one path. The before-and-after is the artefact I’d show first from this project.

I built the system with the pages, not after them. Type scale, colour, buttons and their states, navigation and footer for desktop and mobile, CTA blocks, the language switcher, componentised from the start, because a launch surface with moving product decisions burns designers who hand-craft every page. When trial copy or pricing shifted, the change was a content edit, not a redesign.

I framed features as classroom jobs, not tech specs. The product pages lead with what a teacher does (manage the class, switch lecture mode to discussion mode, keep students engaged), with the 3D render as supporting evidence, not the pitch. For an audience sceptical of metaverse hype, the concrete-job framing was the credibility play.

What happened

The site shipped with the platform’s 2022 launch, in four languages, on the design system above. What I can’t show you is a conversion or traffic number: post-launch measurement wasn’t part of my scope, and this site’s own rule is that a number without a method doesn’t get printed. The evidence this study stands on is the artefacts (the flow rebuild and the system) and the fact that they shipped.
