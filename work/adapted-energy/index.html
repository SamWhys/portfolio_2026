Adapted Energy: making remote delivery credible to an expert audience

An offshore-energy consultancy was selling something its expert buyers instinctively reject: that critical offshore scope can be delivered remotely. Working through an agency, I designed the site that had to make that credible (one navigation serving sceptical clients and the specialists being recruited) and shipped the design system and screens.

In short

  • Offshore energy runs on a small pool of specialists, relocated to site at very high cost per placement. The client's bet: the same expertise delivered remotely, if buyers who know the stakes can be made to believe it.
  • One site had to serve two expert audiences with opposite needs: clients who must find remote delivery credible, and the specialists being recruited to make it possible, plus a third-party job-board integration.
  • I designed the IA, flows, wireframes, screens, and design system, mobile-first, working through an agency.
  • Honest ledger: I never saw post-launch analytics, and the live site diverged from the handoff in visible ways. This study is about the design reasoning and what agency distance costs.

What I owned: the thinking and the screens: IA, flows, wireframes, high-fidelity design, and the design system. Development, the client relationship, and everything after handoff ran through the agency.

The problem, and what was at risk

Offshore energy projects run on a small pool of highly specialised experts, and the industry default is to relocate them to site, often at costs per placement that everyone acknowledges and nobody questions, because the alternative feels unsafe. Nobody in the market thinks relocation is cheap. They think it’s unavoidable.

Adapted Energy’s bet was that it isn’t: the same expertise, delivered remotely, cutting cost and speeding up delivery. But that asks an expert buyer to accept something their instincts reject: that critical offshore scope can be executed without people on site. These are clients who understand the safety and coordination stakes better than anyone, so the site couldn’t win them on cost alone. It had to make remote delivery credible.

And the model only works if the right specialists join. So one site had to serve two expert audiences with opposite needs: a marketing surface convincing clients to switch, and a recruitment surface attracting the talent that makes the promise deliverable, both behind a single navigation, tied into a third-party job board.

Constraints

The defining constraint wasn’t technical. Working as a vendor through an agency meant my access to the client (and to the end users) was rationed. Requirements arrived summarised; questions went back through intermediaries. The integration target (the third-party job board) was fixed, and the audience context argued for mobile-first: specialists checking roles are not reliably at desks.

What I decided, and what I didn’t

One navigation, two journeys. The rejected option was to split the audiences: a corporate site and a separate careers portal. I kept them in one structure (About / What We Do / Our Way / Our Community / Jobs, with client and candidate account entries) because the audiences validate each other: a client evaluating remote delivery is reassured by visible depth of talent; a specialist deciding whether to join is reassured by visible client seriousness. Splitting them would have split the proof.

Credibility-led, not cost-led. The cheaper-faster pitch was the obvious lead and I subordinated it deliberately: the page structure leads with capability, the community of experts, and how delivery actually works, with cost as the consequence rather than the headline. An expert audience hears a cost-first pitch as corner-cutting.

A system, not just screens. The handoff included the component system (logo usage, colour, navigation states, footer, banners, button states, and complete form design for the candidate portal including error states and a password-strength affordance) because a separate development team was building it and post-handoff access was going to be thin. The system was the insurance policy.

What shipped, honestly

The site went live and is the client’s public face today. But the live build diverged from the handoff in ways worth naming rather than hiding: the headline changed, sections were renamed (What We Do became Capability and Services), and the client/candidate account entry points visible in my design aren’t in the shipped navigation. Through an agency, you ship influence, not control: the system and screens I handed off shaped what exists, and someone else decided what survived.

I also never saw post-launch analytics. That’s why this study has no evidence ledger: no number I could print here would be mine to defend. The client accepted the work and shipped on its foundation. That’s the outcome I can state, and the limit of what I’ll claim.

What I’d do differently

Contract the feedback loop, not just the deliverables. The distance the agency model imposed (limited direct contact with the client and no contact with end users) produced bottlenecks and rework that direct access would have prevented, and it’s why the divergences above happened without me in the room. Since then, on in-house projects, I’ve treated access to users and decision-makers as a condition of the work, agreed up front: the same lesson the VISTA study on this site tells from the other side.
