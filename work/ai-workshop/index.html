Leveraging AI: Transforming my team's workflows

My team was adopting AI unevenly and nobody could say what it was worth. I built three working implementations against real project bottlenecks, measured what each one changed, and turned the results into an internal talk that made the case with evidence instead of enthusiasm.

In short

  • AI adoption on the team was uneven and mostly anecdotal. The honest problem was not access to tools, it was that nobody could say which parts of the work AI actually improved.
  • Rather than run a training session, I picked three real bottlenecks on live projects and built a working implementation for each: prototyping, usability analysis, and site-wide auditing.
  • Each implementation carries its own evidence label. The prototyping gain is a directional estimate from my own before-and-after; the audit findings are deterministic axe-core output; the analysis gains are qualitative.
  • I built the talk as an offline, dependency-free presentation engine so it runs from a filesystem with no server, no build step and no network calls, which also made it publishable later.

The problem

AI had arrived on the team the way it arrives everywhere: unevenly, through individual curiosity, with no shared account of what it was good for. People were either quietly using it for everything or quietly avoiding it, and both camps were arguing from anecdote.

The framing I pushed back on was “we should train the team on AI”. That assumes the gap is skill. The actual gap was evidence. Nobody could point at a piece of work and say this part got faster and here is what it cost us. Until someone could, adoption would stay a matter of temperament.

What I decided to do instead

Pick three real bottlenecks on live projects, build a working implementation for each, and report what happened, including where it did not help.

The through-line I set for the whole thing was efficiency without losing effectiveness, with the human in the loop. That is a constraint, not a slogan: it meant any implementation that produced faster output but worse judgement counted as a failure, and had to be reported as one.

01 · Rapid prototyping

A team was reviewing designs for an OKR-writing flow. The bottleneck was the gap between a critique and something a stakeholder could actually try.

I used Figma Make to go from heuristic evaluation to an interactive prototype of the redesigned flow, with the status toggle, a clearer validation action, and the locked states all working. What had been a roughly three-week turnaround became a day or two (F-03).

The judgement did not move. The prototype encoded decisions I had already made in the evaluation; AI compressed the distance to a testable artefact, it did not decide what to build.

02 · Rapid usability analysis

Three moderated, in-person rounds, roughly ten to twelve participants each, measuring SUS alongside open-ended feedback. The bottleneck was the back half: clustering raw feedback, finding themes, and writing it up.

AI took the clustering and first-pass synthesis. I kept the calls: which themes were real, which were one loud participant, and what to recommend. The gain here is qualitative and I have labelled it that way — the work got faster and I would not claim more than that.

03 · Site intelligence

The largest of the three, and the one that produced a tool rather than a document. Two live questions: how far had the business design system actually rolled out, and where did the site stand against WCAG 2.2 AA.

Both had previously been answered by manual hunting. I built two dashboards instead:

  • A rollout audit that detects design-system token adoption in rendered CSS and scores each page for completeness, so “how far along are we” has a reproducible answer. Fifteen of sixteen sampled pages carried nothing beyond the shared chrome tokens (F-05).
  • An accessibility review that runs axe-core against rendered pages, so findings arrive with rule IDs and node counts rather than opinions. Eight pages were audited deterministically (F-04); the rest are marked heuristic in the tool itself.

The part I care about is the labelling. Every figure in both dashboards is tagged measured, assessed, or not measured. A tool that quietly mixes a real axe violation with a judgement call is worse than no tool, because it teaches people to trust the wrong numbers.

The talk, and why it is built the way it is

I built the presentation as a self-contained offline engine: vanilla HTML, CSS and JavaScript, classic script tags, no framework, no bundler, no network calls at runtime. It runs by opening a file.

That was a practical constraint — a talk that depends on a live connection fails in the room it matters in — but it had a second benefit. Because it has no build step and no external dependencies, the same artefact could later be de-identified and published, which is what you are reading about now.

What I would do differently

The efficiency figures are the weak point and I want to be straight about it. F-01, F-02 and F-03 are my own before-and-after estimates, labelled directional for exactly that reason. I had the opportunity to instrument this properly — to track time on a handful of comparable tasks before and after — and I did not. The audit numbers are solid because a tool produced them. The speed numbers are experienced, not measured, and a hostile reader is right to weigh them accordingly.

The other thing: I presented three wins. I did not present a case where AI made the work worse, because on these three it did not, but a talk without a failure in it is a talk that is slightly selling. Next time I would run one implementation specifically to find the ceiling.
